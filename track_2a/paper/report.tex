\begin{abstract}
\noindent Swiss voters meet campaign claims in many languages, while the authoritative source is the
official voting booklet. We present \emph{Büchli}, a system that decides whether a booklet
(German, French or Italian PDF) \emph{supports}, \emph{contradicts} or \emph{does not settle} a claim
in any of the three languages, and quotes booklet sentences with their pages. A small CPU embedder
selects 16 passages and ranks quotations; Apertus\,v1.5 is the only model that decides and cites. On 976 held-out test
claims, Apertus-v1.5-8B reaches macro-F1 \textbf{0.891} (95\% CI 0.871--0.909) with $\sim$3{,}400
prompt tokens per claim; on a paired 60-claim subsample it scores 0.861 against 0.735 for reading the
whole booklet at 31{,}400 tokens. With the organisers' scorer, the same pipeline with
Apertus-v1.5-70B reaches \textbf{0.930} with booklets and \textbf{0.970} with given passages on the
held-out test claims (vote-date-clustered 95\% CI 0.909--0.950 and 0.952--0.983), at the same input
tokens. The repository includes a demo, hosted
publicly during the hackathon, and an explorer of all 976 test answers. Of three evidence configurations, sentence quotes match the
annotated passages best (0.758, against 0.535 for whole pages).
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Swiss voters decide several times a year on complex legal, social and financial proposals, and
during each campaign they meet claims that may or may not match the official booklet, often in another
language than the one they read. An informed vote depends on checking such a claim against the
source, and a language model that misreads it, for example a legal sentence with a qualification or a
negation, is a real risk for democratic decisions. We call the system \emph{Büchli}, the Swiss name
for the voting booklet (\emph{Abstimmungsbüchli}) that every household receives. A typical case: a French claim says a tax applies from a turnover of
500 million euros, while the German booklet says 750 million (p.\,21); Büchli answers
\emph{contradicts} and quotes that sentence (more cases in Appendix~\ref{app:cases}). We ask three
research questions:
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{RQ\arabic*}]
\item Does Apertus understand a booklet across German, French and Italian, without translation?
\item How much of the booklet must it read, and does it find the passage that settles the claim?
\item At what granularity (pages, passages, sentences) should evidence be quoted?
\end{enumerate}
\textbf{Contributions.} (1) A retrieval-then-verify pipeline in which Apertus is the only decision
model, packaged for the organisers' evaluation contract. (2) A leakage-free split of the OST benchmark
by vote date, with a single held-out test run. (3) Controlled comparisons of context size, evidence
granularity, model size (8B vs 70B) and embedder cost, scored with the organisers' own scorer.
(4) A demo and an explorer of all 976 test answers with their
cited pages (Fig.~\ref{fig:demo}; code in \texttt{modal/demo.py}, explorer \texttt{docs/results.html}). (5) All predictions, run files and figures published for re-analysis.

\section{Related work}
NLI classifies a hypothesis as entailed, contradicted or neutral given a premise~\cite{bowman2015snli},
across languages in XNLI~\cite{conneau2018xnli}; FEVER~\cite{thorne2018fever} verifies claims against
retrieved evidence sentences. Our premise is a whole booklet (16--144 pages) in another language than the
claim. We follow retrieval-augmented generation~\cite{lewis2020rag} with a multilingual sentence
embedder~\cite{reimers2020}; layout-aware parsing (Docling~\cite{auer2024docling}) and visual page
retrieval (ColPali~\cite{faysse2024colpali}) are alternatives we test in Appendix~\ref{app:visual}.

\section{Task and data}
\textbf{Task.} Document + claim $\rightarrow$ entailment / neutral / contradiction, judged against the
booklet only, not real-world truth. The official evaluation runs our Docker image on a private set
with two tasks: \emph{A}, booklet PDF + vote name + claim; \emph{B}, a given reference passage +
claim~\cite{starter,ostslides}. All $3\times3$ combinations of claim and booklet language (DE, FR, IT) occur.
\textbf{Data.} The OST benchmark~\cite{ostdataset} has 1{,}488 pairs with approximately balanced
labels, covering all $3\times3$ claim/booklet language combinations (147--196 each); booklets come from the Federal Chancellery~\cite{booklets}. It ships without a split,
so we split \emph{by vote date}, keeping the three language versions of a booklet on the same side:
dev 6 dates / 512 pairs (all design choices), test 14 dates / 976 pairs (run once).

\section{Method}
\begin{figure}[h]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{fig-pipeline.pdf}
\caption{\textbf{The Büchli pipeline.} Booklet and claim may be in different languages; only the 16
passages closest to the claim reach Apertus, which returns the label and the passages it relied on;
verbatim sentences with their pages are quoted as evidence. Editable source:
\texttt{paper/pipeline.excalidraw} (open at excalidraw.com).}
\label{fig:pipeline}
\end{figure}
\textbf{Steps} (Fig.~\ref{fig:pipeline}). \emph{Split:} PyMuPDF~\cite{pymupdf} extracts each page,
rejoins words broken across lines and merges text blocks into passages of about 900 characters (195 of
7{,}610 longer) that never cross a page and keep the PDF outline path (vote $\rightarrow$ part
$\rightarrow$ subsection). \emph{Search:} \texttt{paraphrase-multilingual-MiniLM-L12-v2}~\cite{reimers2020,minilmcard}
(fastembed~\cite{fastembed}) embeds claim and passages in one cross-lingual space; the 16 nearest go to
Apertus in document order. \emph{Decide:} one call, temperature 0, JSON schema \{\texttt{reason},
\texttt{evidence}, \texttt{label}\}; the prompt defines the labels, names the vote and treats a
statement attributed to the wrong actor as a contradiction. \emph{Quote:} up to five verbatim sentences
with pages, three from the cited passages, then two from the rest of the context. The Docker image
follows the evaluation contract (CPU only, no runtime downloads) and uses
Apertus-v1.5-70B~\cite{apertus70b} when served, else the 8B~\cite{apertus8b}.

\section{Experimental setup}
\textbf{Metrics.} Macro-F1 over the three labels (primary); evidence Hit@5 with the organisers'
matcher (a quote counts if it fuzzily lies inside the gold passage or contains it); input tokens per
case; non-LLM processing time. 95\% intervals from 2{,}000 bootstrap resamples of claims.
\textbf{Official scoring}~\cite{ostslides}. Valid only if macro-F1 $\geq$ 0.60 (A) and $\geq$ 0.75 (B; slide 16, while the starter's
\texttt{evaluate.py} prints 0.70).
Task A $=0.5\,$F1 $+\,0.2\,$Hit@5 $+\,0.15\,$time $+\,0.15\,$tokens; task B $=0.7\,$F1 $+\,0.15\,$time
$+\,0.15\,$tokens; final $=0.6A+0.4B$; time is wall-clock minus time with an LLM request in flight,
and both efficiency terms are relative to the best team.
\textbf{Baselines.} Whole booklet in the prompt; $k=8$ passages; two alternative neural embedders and
BM25 fusion.

\textbf{RQ1, languages.} On test, cross-lingual pairs score 0.899 (n\,=\,641) and same-language pairs
0.875 (n\,=\,335): no \emph{average} penalty. The average hides a weakness (Fig.~\ref{fig:errors}): on
contradiction claims that mention an actor, the 8B errs in 14 of 107 cross-lingual cases against 2 of 41
same-language ones (small numbers). Claims say ``the committee'', ``le comité'' or ``il comitato'', while
the booklet names a specific body in another language (``comitati referendari'',
``Initiativkomitee''); in 11 of the 14 the right passage was in context and the model answered
\emph{neutral}. Reading these cases suggests that mapping actors across languages, rather than
translation as such, is the open problem. A prompt glossary of actor names across the three languages did
not fix it (actor claims 82/90 before and after with the 8B; 70B task B 0.958 against 0.979--0.986, at
+137 tokens), and neither did asking Apertus to name the actors before the label (70B dev task A 0.865
against 0.918), so neither is used.

\textbf{RQ2, context.} Sixteen retrieved passages beat the whole booklet at a ninth of the tokens
(Fig.~\ref{fig:context}). Halving the context ($k=8$) saves 45\% of tokens but costs F1 (8B dev 0.850
vs 0.890) and evidence (70B 0.677 vs 0.828), so we keep $k=16$.
Given the annotated passage instead (task B), the 8B scores 0.930 against 0.857: of its 21 task-A
errors, 14 disappear, 7 remain and 3 new ones appear, so errors persist even with the reference text;
separating retrieval, context interference and reasoning needs case-level analysis. Contradiction read as neutral is the main confusion (31 of 106 test
errors); retrieval misses explain 26 of the 106.

\textbf{RQ3, evidence.} Three evidence configurations score 0.535 (whole pages), 0.616 (passages) and
0.758 (sentences) under the official matcher (Fig.~\ref{fig:evid}a). The comparison is not fully
controlled: the sentence configuration comes from a separate inference run (two task-A labels differ)
and also adds sentences from uncited context.
\textbf{Model size.} With identical prompts, retrieval and input tokens (output 92 against 86), the 70B
gains 6 points on the booklet task on dev (mean 0.918 over four runs, range 0.905--0.932) and holds on
the held-out test split: 0.930 against 0.884 for the 8B in the same format, with evidence Hit@5 0.844
against 0.799. Given the passage (task B), both are near the ceiling on test (8B 0.979, 70B 0.970), so the
dev advantage there does not carry over (Table~\ref{tab:main}).
\textbf{Embedder cost.} Larger embedders retrieve better but cost 25--60$\times$ the CPU time
(Fig.~\ref{fig:evid}b); the submitted image spends 0.22\,s of non-LLM time per case.
\textbf{Inference cost.} One Apertus call per claim; end-to-end median 3.9\,s (8B) and
4.9\,s (70B) per booklet claim. At Modal list prices and our measured throughput (warm server, 6 parallel
requests) the GPU costs about \$0.46 per 1{,}000 claims for the 8B (one L40S) and \$3.75--6.48 for the
70B (two H200).

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:learned}
\textbf{What we learned.} The scorer, not intuition, decides what good evidence is: our first
instinct, and that of another AI we asked, was to cite whole pages, and measuring showed the opposite.
\textbf{Hackathon scoring versus real use.} The scorer charges LLM tokens but not LLM waiting time,
and it charges CPU time; hence the 70B and the small embedder. A real service would weigh differently:
users wait for each answer and the 70B costs more GPU time, while booklets could be embedded once in
advance, making stronger embedders cheap.
\textbf{Limitations.} Reruns vary by up to 0.027 macro-F1; choices rest on 6 dev vote dates; Hit@5 is a proxy and two of five quotes come from uncited context; retrieval can
pick the wrong vote in a multi-vote booklet; PyMuPDF reads tables as lines.

\section{Ethics, use of AI and reproducibility}
\textbf{Ethics.} A verdict states what the booklet says, not whether a claim is true or how to vote:
a model prediction to check against the cited page, not advice. The demo processes visitor IPs only for
rate limits and stores no claims.
\textbf{Use of AI.} Apertus decides and MiniLM retrieves. Development used Claude Code (Claude Opus
5.5) for implementation and experiments under the author's direction, plus read-only reviews by Codex
and Grok; every finding was checked against the code and every number against \texttt{runs/}.
\textbf{Reproducibility.} Code, run files and all predictions are in the repository (Apache-2.0);
dataset revision \texttt{fc2b276}; temperature 0, seed 0. Every experiment, including the rejected ones, is in the archival \texttt{technical\_report.md}.

\section{Conclusion and future work}\label{sec:future}
We set out to see whether Apertus can help voters check what the official booklet really says, even
when the claim and the booklet are in different languages. It can: given a claim, it finds the passage
that matters, gives a verdict and points to the page, and it judges more than nine in ten claims correctly on
votes it has never seen. The most useful lessons were less about the model than about how it is used:
reading less of the booklet works better than reading all of it, short quotes are better evidence than
whole pages, and the hardest part is not translation but knowing who said what.

\textbf{RQ1, languages.} All $3\times3$ combinations of the three languages work without translation (cross-lingual 0.899,
same-language 0.875); actor-mentioning contradictions fail more often across languages (14 of 107
against 2 of 41), and a prompt glossary of actor names did not fix it.
\textbf{RQ2, context.} Reading the 16 retrieved passages instead of the whole booklet is both more
accurate (0.861 against 0.735 on the same 60 claims) and uses 89\% fewer input tokens (3{,}446 against 31{,}402
input tokens). On the 976 held-out claims, the 70B reaches macro-F1 0.930 against 0.884 for the 8B in the
official format, at identical input tokens. Only 26 of the 106 test errors are retrieval misses; the rest are decisions on retrieved text,
which we think fine-tuning can address.
\textbf{RQ3, evidence.} Under the organisers' matcher, single verbatim sentences match the annotated
evidence best (0.758, against 0.616 for passages and 0.535 for whole pages).
The system meets the evaluation contract with one Apertus call per claim, at about \$0.46 (8B) to
\$6.48 (70B) of GPU time per 1{,}000 claims.

\textbf{Future work.} Actor-aware verification tested on minimal pairs; distilling the 70B into the 8B
on more vote dates; visual page retrieval (ColQwen2 finds the gold page in the top 5 for 93.5\% of
claims against 71.3\%, Appendix~\ref{app:visual}); and serving voters who never read the booklet,
opinion surveys and political barometers, cantonal votes and other countries.
